# Chasing wind and sun narrows but does not close the data-centre clean-power gap

> Readable text snapshot. The authoritative submitted file is `main_text_submission.docx`.

## Abstract

Data centres are the fastest-growing electrical load. Within them, the share driven by real-time user interaction is fixed to local dusk by human routines. Solar output is fixed to local noon by Earth's geometry. The two sit about eight hours apart. Relocating such load along longitudes toward daylight, so that following the sun bypasses the local storage bottleneck, has drawn high hopes. Yet how far the temporal structure of national load can align in phase with clean supply, at the planetary scale, has not been quantified. We couple hourly human web traffic across 104 countries with worldwide station-level wind and solar output. Supply is held to each country's own territory, and reach is gated by real network round-trip latency. Under equal-energy normalisation we characterise the phase-alignability between load and reachable clean supply. With domestic wind and solar alone, the median country's hourly wind-and-solar matching gap is about four-tenths. The deficit falls without exception in the evening. Cross-longitude routing compresses this gap sharply but never closes it. Even when load is almost fully relocatable and latency is relaxed to cross-hemisphere global reach near 500 ms, the median gap holds at about one-eighth. This residual is set by the phase offset between human routines and Earth's rotation, and does not vanish with better scheduling. What limits further reduction is not network latency, which saturates within a few hundred milliseconds, but the share of load that can be relocated. Reshaping the demand curve by AI inference matters just as little. The cost of firming this residual with domestic storage, the phase-firming premium, is likewise set by routing reach. Global reach stays below on-site, yet as clean generation cheapens faster than storage, the relative weight of this phase mismatch grows rather than fades. Global low-carbon computing thus meets a phase-alignability boundary, set jointly by human routines and Earth's rotation. Its implication is not boundless capacity expansion inside one national grid. It is instead the siting of data centres to follow the temporal structure of the world's clean supply.

The electricity footprint of the digital economy is climbing fast. The International Energy Agency puts global data-centre demand at about 415 TWh in 2024, rising to about 945 TWh by 2030<sup>1,2</sup>. That already exceeds what Japan consumes today. Decarbonisation standards are tightening in parallel. Google, Microsoft and the United Nations 24/7 Carbon-Free Energy Compact have moved the target from annual energy offsetting to hourly matching<sup>3</sup>. Whether every hour of compute demand can be met by carbon-free power has become a central question where digital infrastructure meets the power system.

Research on this hourly mismatch has taken two paths. Grid-side studies lower the matching cost through portfolio optimisation of storage and generation<sup>4</sup>. Compute load, by contrast, is seen to hold a spatial flexibility that other industries lack<sup>5,6</sup>. The logic of follow-the-sun scheduling is appealing. The sun always lights some part of the Earth, and data move at near light speed. Load can therefore chase clean output worldwide, so that diurnal mismatches offset one another. This idea has already spawned many carbon-aware scheduling algorithms and pilots at leading cloud providers<sup>5,6,7</sup>. Global routing is now seen as a key path to hourly carbon-free computing.

Observation and expectation, however, pull apart. Solar electricity has fallen by about 90% in a decade, and network latency is long since low enough. Yet the hourly carbon-free share of leading providers has hovered near two-thirds for years. Procurement access and grid structure explain part of this, but the persistence also hints at a more basic structural limit on temporal matching. The efficacy of follow-the-sun scheduling rests on one untested premise<sup>8</sup>. It assumes that national demand waveforms are offset enough in longitude for one region's evening deficit to meet another's midday surplus. Prior work has mostly treated the timing of load and carbon intensity at the regional level<sup>5,6,7</sup>. Interactive compute load has never been metered country by country and hour by hour. How tightly each demand peak lags the local supply peak, and whether demand is concentrated or dispersed along longitude, both remain unknown. Together these two quantities decide how far diurnal mismatches can cancel. Both are fixed by the geometry of land across longitude. Even with clean power in ample supply and migration made costless, it is unknown how low global routing can drive the hourly gap. It is equally unknown how large a phase residual survives under an equal-energy wind-and-solar baseline with no overbuild.

To answer this, we assemble three datasets — hourly digital-activity traffic for 104 countries<sup>9</sup>, full-year hourly output for 461 representative wind and solar stations<sup>10</sup>, and a global matrix of network round-trip latency<sup>8</sup>. From these we build a framework of phase-alignability. Supply is confined to domestic wind and solar, equal-energy normalisation makes clean supply exactly cover demand in total, and migration is set costless. Capacity and network limits are thus ceded in advance to the follow-the-sun side. Any residual gap can then be attributed only to the phase relation between demand and supply waveforms on a common clock. The measurement supports the hypothesis. In 99 of 104 countries the demand peak lags the domestic supply peak by about 8 hours. The global digital evening is nearly synchronous, and the longitude offset that follow-the-sun assumes does not hold. The consequence is direct. Even under the setting most favourable to follow-the-sun, ideal global routing lowers the median matching gap only from 40.1% to 12.7% and cannot reach zero. Because capacity and network limits were ceded in advance, this value is an optimistic lower bound on the wind-and-solar basis. Real-world constraints can only widen it. Follow-the-sun works, yet the residual it cannot reach must be pre-covered by firm clean generation or long-duration storage.

## Normalized measures of temporal mismatch

Directly accounting for each country's hourly carbon-free supply gap is not yet feasible. That load is not metered country by country and hour by hour. Independent estimates of its absolute energy differ several-fold. Yet the question posed here does not depend on absolute magnitude. Whether digital demand and clean supply align in time is a property fixed only by the phase relation between two within-day waveforms.

We therefore rescale each country's digital-activity and clean-output series to the same annual total, discard the information on volume, and keep only temporal structure. We call this property phase-alignability, and quantify it with two measures. The hourly wind-and-solar matching gap \(U_c\) is the share of demand that, under equal-energy normalisation, cannot be met by contemporaneous clean supply, and it is provably identical to the hourly matching score of the 24/7 carbon-free energy (CFE) framework, now standard in corporate clean-power procurement and policy<sup>3,4</sup>. The phase-firming premium \(\Pi_c\) is the increment, over pure wind-and-solar generation cost, of firming the residual gap with storage, the standard measure used in studies of variable-renewable integration cost<sup>11,12</sup>. \(U_c\) captures the physical share of the phase mismatch and \(\Pi_c\) its economic cost, and the two are driven by the same mismatch. Their formal definitions are given in the Methods.

This construction is robust to uncertainty in volume. Wherever the absolute level of AI energy use falls within current projections, the normalised phase relation is unchanged. Demand volume depends on technological progress and adoption, whereas the phase structure is set by Earth's rotation and human routines. The latter is what this study measures.

## Results

### Domestic wind and solar alone leave the median country a 40.1% hourly wind-and-solar matching gap

The hourly match between global digital demand and domestic wind-plus-solar supply exhibits a consistent directional misalignment across all densely populated latitude bands, and no single country can absorb the resulting gap under equal-energy installed capacity. The median hourly wind-and-solar matching gap U across the 104 countries is 40.1%, reaching a maximum of 55.5% in Palestine and a minimum of 16.8% in the United States; 94 of the 104 countries exceed 25% and 22 exceed 50% (Fig. 1a). The Africa/MENA and Asia-Pacific regions concentrate most of the worst-mismatched countries, led by Bangladesh at 55%, Cambodia 55%, Saudi Arabia 54% and Algeria 54%, indicating that hourly mismatch is not a single climatic problem of high or equatorial latitudes but a structural tension between the temporal structure of digital society and the physical rhythm of renewables.

The direction of the mismatch is highly consistent and is driven primarily by the intraday temporal offset between demand and supply. Folded by local hour, human web-access traffic in almost every country forms a pronounced evening peak at local 18:00–22:00, whereas domestic renewable supply forms a midday peak at local 10:00–14:00 and falls back sharply at 18:00–23:00, so the demand–supply peak phase difference in the median country is about 8 hours (Fig. 1c). This phase difference is equally robust at the country level — solar PV locks the supply peak of the vast majority of countries at 12:00–13:00, whereas the demand peak is broad and late, clustered at 18:00–22:00, and in 99/104 countries the demand peak lags the supply peak.

The evening is precisely where the gap lies. The difference between demand and supply (Fig. 1c, right axis) is pushed negative at midday by the solar surplus and, after 18:00–23:00, reverses into the largest deficit of the day; tiling the 104 countries into a matrix from bottom to top by matching gap U (Fig. 1b), the same evening bright band aligns horizontally across all countries, spanning every region and time zone, and in most countries the hourly deficit within this window reaches above 60% of annual mean demand, exceeding 100% in the worst-affected countries.

Differences among countries lie only in the magnitude of the deficit; its evening timing is universal—sorted in ascending order of matching gap U, the evening deficit merely deepens monotonically along the ordering, its position never shifting or disappearing, and all 104 countries concentrate the bulk of their all-day deficit in the evening (a median of 66.7% falling in 18:00–23:00, about 2.7 times the uniform baseline; Fig. 1b). Where a country sits along this gradient is set mainly by its domestic supply mix. The closer its supply is to pure solar, the higher its matching gap U, and the share of wind in wind-plus-solar capacity is strongly negatively correlated with U (Spearman ρ = −0.61, p < 10⁻⁹), because solar output is locked at midday by astronomical geometry and its offset from evening-peaking demand cannot be evaded. Wind-rich countries can soften this phase difference but cannot move where the gap falls—even in the United States, where the demand–supply phase difference is not pronounced, the deficit still concentrates in the evening window (Fig. 1b, leftmost column).

The clock-term gap, recomputed from each country's 24-hour mean intraday profile alone, explains about three-quarters of the cross-country variation in the annual gap (R² = 0.74, Fig. 1d)—intraday temporal offset, not weather fluctuation, drives the divergence in magnitude among countries; weather and seasonal fluctuations only shift the overall level systematically upward, by a median of about 5.7 percentage points (IQR 2–12). Country-level detail on the matching gap U, the demand–supply peak phase difference and the 95th-percentile continuous gap duration is given in Supplementary Table S17.

Migrating compute load to regions that momentarily still have clean output compresses the 24/7 CFE hourly gap without relying on cross-border grids. The residual reported below is a single-country marginal measure, solved independently for each country with supply held fixed domestically and ignoring cross-country surplus contention. When load runs entirely on-site, the median country's matching gap U is about four-tenths, in line with the 40.1% installed-capacity headline of Fig. 1; the cross-longitude analysis is solved on each country's domestic wind-and-solar portfolio, whose no-routing baseline sits within 2 percentage points of that headline (Methods). Once load is allowed to migrate to countries that still have clean output within its round-trip-time budget, the gap falls as the relocatable share and the tolerable latency relax together; but even when the share is fully open and latency is relaxed to cross-hemisphere global reach under a ~500 ms network round-trip budget, the median residual settles at about 12.7% (Fig. 2a) and falls no further.

The gain from relaxing the latency budget tops out rapidly at a few hundred milliseconds. Inter-country round-trip time reaches at most about 340 ms, so a single request need only tolerate about 200 to 300 ms to reach clean supply at almost any longitude, and wider budgets—even second-scale queuing—bring no further improvement (Fig. 2a). When load is fully routable, expanding reach from local to about 3000 km compresses the median gap only to 22.9%, and only further expansion that crosses to the other hemisphere drives it sharply down to about 12.7% (Fig. 2b). Neighbouring countries share the same diurnal phase and fall into night together; only crossing larger longitudinal spans to meet far-side resources still in daylight yields substantial complementarity. Which class of load falls in which latency tier is set by the round-trip budget a single request actually leaves for the network once on-server compute is subtracted. Voice assistants, code completion and real-time retrieval-augmented generation retain only tens of milliseconds after compute and cannot even reach the nearest neighbour, so they are absorbed on-site, whereas image and video generation and batch inference—whose compute far exceeds any trans-oceanic round trip—leave an effectively unbounded network budget and can be routed to any longitude. The measured service-level targets, compute times and assigned latency tiers of these workload classes are given in Supplementary Section S18.

What limits the gap's descent is not network latency but how much load can be served off-site. A substantial share of digital load is locked on-site by proximity access, stateful sessions and data residency. Under global reach, each increment in the off-site-servable load share lowers the median gap U nearly linearly, falling from 34.6% at one-fifth through 27.8%, 22.4% and 16.7% to 12.7% when fully open (Fig. 2a, right margin), and to about 27.8% at the realistic routable share of about four-tenths. Approaching this floor requires services to be designed almost entirely stateless and off-site-servable, an architectural undertaking that relaxing latency cannot substitute for.

The power-sourcing network that realizes this potential is highly dispersed, and the gains come overwhelmingly from crossing longitudes rather than from regional interconnection. Routed load flows into about 38 clean-generation timing partners—whose clean-output timing complements the sender's evening peak, with capacity not modelled—led chiefly by Mexico, Chile, Australia, Japan, South Africa, Spain and the United States, dispersed along different longitudes rather than converging on a few nearby sources; these partners are dominated by low-latitude high-irradiance solar PV, with mid- and high-latitude wind secondary, spanning all longitude bands (Fig. 3g,h). Regional-scale reach captures only a small part of the gain, and the real compression occurs at the step that reaches the other hemisphere still in daylight — as the latency budget relaxes from regional reach of about 3000 km (Fig. 3a,b) progressively to trans-hemispheric and global (Fig. 3e–h), the power-sourcing pairings open from nearby neighbours to clean-generation timing partners across all longitudes, and the cross-national routed share rises from about 14% to about 27%.

This dispersed configuration lowers the gap because it lets each country's evening peak be carried in relay by solar PV still at midday elsewhere. Whatever the local hour at which a country's digital demand peaks, its routed load almost always runs around solar noon in the receiving country, and the energy-weighted mean landing solar time of each country's routed load clusters around 13:00, nearly independent of the local hour of the demand peak (Fig. 2c). Countries peaking in the evening therefore reach back westward by about 8 solar hours on average to source power, exactly offsetting the roughly 8-hour phase lag by which their demand peak trails the domestic wind-and-solar output peak.

Geographic routing can compress the gap but cannot eliminate it. Even with load fully routable and latency relaxed to cross-hemisphere global reach under a ~500 ms network round-trip budget, the median country's matching gap U is still about 12.7%, concentrated in resource-poor grids whose domestic gaps were already the largest, closable only by local firm clean generation, long-duration storage or physical relocation of data centres. Relocating compute itself can be deployed on the existing internet without building new cross-border grids.

The effect of AI load on 24/7 CFE lies not in the total energy it injects but in how it reshapes the within-day profile of human digital demand once a flat training baseload is superimposed. As the inference and training shares rise, the demand curve is partly flattened and an inference peak is added in the evening (Fig. 4a). Yet this shape effect in itself exerts little influence on the residual gap. Under fixed domestic supply, reshaped demand merely redistributes an equal amount of energy among hours, and both the night trough it fills and the evening peak it raises fall within the deficit window where there is no sunlight and clean supply is already near zero; against the supply trough dominated by solar PV, the gains and losses of peak-shaving and valley-filling nearly cancel (Fig. 4b). As a result, the matching gap U under the expected mixed-AI profile differs from the AI-free baseline by only about 1 percentage point, the six AI profiles lie close to one another, and this is far smaller than the routing drop between domestic and global reach (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Fig. S4). What determines the magnitude of the matching gap U and the phase-firming premium Π is therefore not the shape of the reshaped demand curve, but how far the compute load can be routed and what fraction of it can be taken up elsewhere (Fig. 4d).

The cost dividend and the gap compression arise from the same cross-longitude sharing of clean supply's timing. We measure this by the phase-firming premium Π, the fraction that must be paid on top of pure wind-and-solar generation cost to firm the residual phase gap with each country's own storage—the cost-language mirror of the matching gap U, computed per country on domestic supply, not a complete cross-national compute-deployment cost. When load can only be executed on site, the median country must pay about 61% above generation cost to close its domestic evening gap; once cross-site routing is allowed, this premium declines monotonically as reach expands, falling to about 55% at full routability with cross-hemisphere reach (Fig. 5a). The equivalent firming-cost share likewise narrows from about 38% locally to about 36% globally. Receiving countries contribute only the shape (waveform) of their clean output, not cross-border energy or cost — the same waveform can enter the basis of several sending countries without double-counting any one unit of power (Methods). Whether receiving-side renewable and compute capacity suffice, and how the finite surplus is apportioned, are deliverability questions—of quantity and contention—placed, like the matching gap U, outside the scope of this study.

This decline in premium comes almost entirely from saved firming storage. With supply fixed domestically, moving load to where clean supply exists at that moment cuts the residual phase gap that storage must fill across time by about a quarter—what shrinks is the shape of the gap itself, not any transported energy—so the firming-storage premium falls from about 57% of generation cost locally to about 50% globally, accounting for most of the decline in Π, while the ancillary-services premium stays at about 4% (Fig. 5a,b). The phase-firming premium is thus in essence an integration cost of phase mismatch—the better the phase alignment and the lower the matching gap, the fewer gap segments storage must bridge and the closer the premium falls to zero.

The direction of the trend is worth emphasising. Although absolute levelized cost falls year by year as solar PV, wind and battery costs decline, the phase-firming premium does not fall but rises—the full-routability global-reach tier climbs from about 47% in 2025 to about 62% in 2050, and the on-site tier from about 53% to about 68% (Fig. 5c,d). The reason is that clean generation becomes ever cheaper while the storage needed to fill the phase mismatch declines more slowly, so the relative cost of phase mismatch does not recede with technological progress but grows; global reach still stays below on-site every year, yet this phase constraint does not fade with technology, and its relative economic weight instead intensifies. Such routing can be carried by the existing internet without building new cross-border power grids, but its realization at scale remains constrained by the compute capacity at the receiving location and by transoceanic bandwidth; the residual gap that routing cannot eliminate must still be closed by local firm clean generation and long-duration storage.

## Discussion

This work pushes a paradigm to its edge. The field has long treated the mismatch between load and clean supply through spatial aggregation. Hirth traced the integration cost of variable renewables to the temporal shape of output<sup>11,12</sup>. MacDonald and Clack then showed that a wider footprint of geographic interconnection smooths weather fluctuations<sup>13</sup>. Since then, mismatch has largely been read as a stochastic quantity that decays as the aggregation radius grows<sup>14,15</sup>. The near-light-speed migration of compute load is often taken as the limiting case<sup>8</sup>. Our normalised lower bound shows that this decay has a strictly positive limit on the equal-energy wind-and-solar basis. Once normalisation strips the absolute scale of capacity and load, what remains is the phase geometry between two unit waveforms. Demand is locked by human routines into a single global phase. The supply peak is fixed by Earth's rotation and the longitude of land, about eight hours away. However ideal the routing, a share no smaller than this bound always falls in the trough of global clean output. The bound describes a property of temporal shape, not a ban on capacity expansion. Overbuilding, and in particular raising the share of wind that generates at night, can compress the gap beyond the equal-energy constraint. Its price is curtailment and cost, a separate axis of trade-off.

The phase constraint still shapes how clean-computing resources should be ranked. Current 24/7 procurement frameworks emphasise flexibility and capacity expansion, and place routing and new clean capacity first<sup>3,4</sup>. Our results show that, within a fixed energy budget, the gap reachable by spatial routing has a geometric ceiling. The remainder can be filled only by time-shifting resources such as long-duration storage or by firm clean generation. Overbuilding can compress the gap further, but its marginal curtailment cost must be weighed against storage country by country, rather than assumed sufficient. In siting data centres and in clean-power purchase agreements, planners should bring this residual share into assessment up front. They should reserve an explicit place for time-shifting resources, and not assume that routing and capacity targets cover every hour unconditionally.

The measurement has limits of its own. Normalisation clarifies the phase geometry but abstracts away the absolute constraint of deliverable power. Real capacity is finite. As several sending countries draw on the same midday partners, contention can only raise the actual mismatch. The bound is therefore a conservative floor, not an attainable target. Using network traffic as a proxy for compute load may understate the batch share that does not follow daily rhythms. It also omits idle server power and cooling. That static base flattens the intraday amplitude of the real power curve, so the measured gap may sit below the traffic-based estimate here. Future work should bring receiving-side compute and wind-solar capacity into a global joint optimisation, and replace the proxy with measured hourly power at the data-centre level. It should also embed the phase floor in a co-expansion model with overbuild and storage, testing how far this geometric constraint weighs on real power-system planning.

## Methods

### Hourly demand–supply panel and equal-energy normalization

The analysis sample is an hourly demand–supply panel for 104 countries spanning 2 May 2025 to 1 May 2026, at 8,760 integer hours per country and roughly 910,000 country–hour observations, with inclusion conditioned on having both Cloudflare human-user traffic and station-level renewable supply records. The demand curve is taken from country-level human web traffic on Cloudflare Radar, a measure of human digital demand—the user-facing, relocatable interactive/inference service load. We model the intraday shape of interactive digital electricity use \(d_c(t)\) as a linear functional of human request rates, \(d_c(t) \propto \sum_k n_k(t)\,e_k\), where \(n_k(t)\) is the arrival rate of request class \(k\) and \(e_k\) its mean energy per request; provided the intraday composition of request classes is approximately stationary, the aggregate request rate \(R_c(t)=\sum_k n_k(t)\) is proportional to the use shape, so \(R_c(t)\) serves as the shape proxy for \(d_c(t)\). Because equal-energy normalisation retains only this shape and divides out the absolute magnitude, the proxy captures the temporal structure of electricity use rather than absolute power; robustness to this stationary-composition assumption and to compute-intensity weighting is shown in Supplementary Section S17. Flat training and batch baseload has no intraday rhythm and is carried separately by the flat training share within the AI operator, never entering its intraday shape. This intraday shape is independently corroborated by the BurstGPT and Azure real-world inference-serving traces and is robust to the flat baseload share, as shown in Supplementary Sections S2, S4 and S17. The supply curve is taken from each country's domestic wind and solar stations — the wind and solar units in the WRI Global Power Plant Database<sup>10</sup> are aggregated on a 3° grid into 455 capacity-weighted representative stations, with one solar and one wind centroid proxy added for each of the three demand countries lacking domestic station records, giving 461 candidate stations in total; each station's Open-Meteo ERA5 meteorology is converted to a station-level hourly capacity factor through the standard PVWatts solar model and IEC wind power curves<sup>16</sup>, and these are then combined by installed-capacity weighting, with station aggregation and conversion parameters given in Supplementary Section S9; because the same station's reanalysis meteorology yields both irradiance and wind speed, every country obtains both solar and wind resource shapes simultaneously<sup>17</sup>. To separate temporal structure from the absolute magnitude of load, the demand and supply curves are each rescaled to the same annual total, so that any residual mismatch reflects only the difference in temporal shape between the two curves, not the absolute level of installed capacity or load.

### Hourly wind-and-solar matching gap

Domestic hourly phase mismatch is captured primarily by the wind-and-solar matching gap \(U_c\), the fraction of annual demand that exceeds contemporaneous supply under the equal-energy condition,

\[ U_c = \frac{\sum_t \max\big(d_c(t)-s_c(t),\,0\big)}{\sum_t d_c(t)} \in [0,1]. \tag{1}\]

Because the demand and supply curves are each rescaled to the same annual total, \(U_c\) reflects only the difference in temporal shape between the two curves. Its complement \(1-U_c=\sum_t \min(d_c(t),s_c(t))/\sum_t d_c(t)\) equals, hour by hour, the 24/7 carbon-free energy (CFE) matching score<sup>3,4</sup>, and likewise the complement of the temporal self-sufficiency rate<sup>18</sup> and of the net-zero-energy-building load match index<sup>19</sup>; under the same equal-energy condition, \(U_c\) is furthermore the total-variation distance between the two normalised shape distributions and \(1-U_c\) their overlap coefficient<sup>20</sup>. These identities let the phase-alignability floor reported here be cross-checked against established procurement, policy and statistical metrics, with the full derivation and a de-normalisation counterexample in Supplementary Section S21. The same mismatch is further characterized from two complementary angles — phase offset, the difference between the intraday peak hours of demand and supply, and persistent deficit, the distribution of run lengths over which demand stays above supply, capturing temporal misalignment and exposure duration respectively.

### Domestic supply portfolio optimization

The domestic supply portfolio is formulated as a non-negative investment problem targeting temporal complementarity<sup>17,21</sup>. Each domestic wind or solar station contributes a capacity-factor basis function normalized to unit mean, stacked into a matrix \(\Phi_c\); the weights of the portfolio curve \(\tilde{s}_c = \Phi_c w_c\) are determined by non-negative least squares so that it tracks demand hour by hour,

\[
\min_{w_c \geq 0}\ \big\| \Phi_c\, w_c - d_c \big\|_2^2. \tag{2}\]

After solving, the weights are rescaled to equal energy, and the residual matching gap of each scenario follows the definition of \(U_c\), with the portfolio curve \(\tilde{s}_c\) substituted for \(s_c\). The headline domestic matching gap is the 40.1% equal-energy installed-capacity mix of Fig. 1. The cross-longitude routing of Result 2 is solved on a domestic station-level portfolio, which lets the optimizer select the stations most temporally complementary to demand; its no-routing on-site baseline is 41.8%, and because non-negative least squares minimizes the squared hourly demand–supply deviation rather than the uncovered energy itself, this is an upper bound. A linear program minimizing uncovered energy directly under the same equal-energy constraint gives a floor of 40.8%. The installed-capacity mix, the non-negative-least-squares portfolio and the linear-program floor agree to within 2 percentage points, so the domestic baseline is robust both to how supply is constructed and to the choice of objective (Supplementary Section S10).

### Scenario design for compute-load routing

Sharing clean resources across borders can move either electricity or compute; this study characterizes the latter—holding each country's supply fixed domestically while routing relocatable compute load over the internet to regions that currently still have clean output<sup>8,14,22,23</sup>. The scenarios are arranged as a two-dimensional matrix of latency tolerance \(\tau\) and routable share \(\varphi\), with six levels on each axis for 36 combinations in total, \(\tau\) from about 50 ms local to about 500 ms global and \(\varphi\) from non-routable to fully routable, solved independently for all 104 countries; its two corners range from the no-routing status-quo baseline to the global phase-alignability upper bound with both axes fully open<sup>15</sup>.

Each country uses only its own wind-and-solar composite supply curve, whose station-level capacity factors are constructed as described above, and does not dispatch electricity across borders; cross-longitude geographic complementarity is realized entirely through the routing of compute load rather than by moving electricity itself.

The routable share \(\varphi\) gives the fraction of load that can be served elsewhere, the remainder being locked locally by proximity access, stateful sessions or data residency<sup>8,24</sup>; the latency tolerance \(\tau\) gates the reachable receiving countries through network round-trip time. The round-trip time between two countries is estimated from the great-circle distance between centroids as \(\mathrm{RTT} = 2(\ell/v + \delta)\,\kappa\), where the propagation speed, per-end switching overhead and wide-area detour factor are taken from existing measurements<sup>8</sup>, with values and validation in Supplementary Section S11. An eligible receiving country must simultaneously satisfy a round-trip time no greater than \(\tau\) and host a hyperscale cloud region, about 39 countries, the rest acting only as senders; cloud-region availability and the receiving-country grouping are given in Supplementary Section S12. Reachability in practice is further shaped by P95/P99 tail latency, cross-cloud egress cost, failover, spare GPU capacity and KV-cache/session-state constraints, which we do not model one by one; the distance-based RTT model already carries a tail-latency margin, however, in that it systematically overestimates the P50 round-trip time of the key long-distance country pairs, as shown in Supplementary Section S11 and Table S19.

### Cross-longitude routing as waveform matching

Moving routable load to regions that currently still have clean output is equivalent to following renewable output and rearranging consumption in space to reduce storage<sup>5,6,25</sup>. Routing is a pure same-hour spatial migration with no cross-hour deferral, because human web-access traffic cannot be delayed by hours. Here \(\tau\) is a network round-trip latency tolerance only and does not include the temporal deferrability of a task—the time window over which its execution may be postponed; interactive load cannot be pushed across hours, so the residual here refers specifically to the same-hour phase gap of interactive load; same-hour execution is a physical property of this load class, not a tunable setting. Genuinely deferrable training and batch jobs are a different load class that can move across time as well as space and would push the gap lower still; relaxing the same-hour convention therefore does not tighten a bound on this residual but turns to a different question, and temporal deferrability is placed outside the scope of this study. The directional effect of each simplifying assumption on this residual is set out item by item in Supplementary Section S15. We model it as timing, or waveform, matching rather than quantity matching — the domestic portfolio is first fitted to demand to obtain the gap still uncovered after optimization, and the routable part of that gap is then covered by the clean-generation shapes of reachable receiving countries. The routable shortfall is the smaller of the load corresponding to the routable share and the domestic gap, and the share of it that reachable partners' clean shapes can cover is fixed by a non-negative least-squares fit,

\[
r_c(t) = \min\!\left(\varphi\, d_c(t),\ \mathrm{deficit}_c(t)\right), \qquad
\mathrm{covered}_c(t) = \min\!\left(r_c(t),\ \sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}_c(\tau)} w_i\,\hat{s}_i(t)\right), \tag{3}\]

where \(\mathcal{R}_c(\tau)\) is the set of receiving countries reachable within \(\tau\) and hosting a cloud region, \(\hat{s}_i\) is receiving country \(i\)'s clean-generation shape equal-energy-normalised to the routable-shortfall energy, and the weights \(w_i \ge 0\) are found by fitting the time-series shape of \(r_c\) by non-negative least squares; their static non-negative combination is the largest share of the shortfall's timing that reachable partners' clean generation can cover, magnitude being divided out at every step by the equal-energy normalisation. To ensure that routing never raises the gap, routing and portfolio optimization are coupled in two steps — the domestic portfolio is first solved for the unrouted demand to obtain the gap, and after routing the portfolio is re-solved for the effective demand.

This routing performs timing, or waveform, matching, not quantity matching — under equal-energy normalisation the question is only whether the shape of reachable partners' clean generation can cover the local shortfall in time, magnitude being divided out at every step. A timing signal is non-rival — one partner's clean-output shape may enter the basis of several sending countries at once without any single share of power being drawn twice, and the residual therefore measures the geometric floor of phase alignability. Delivering this timing potential as actual clean power is subject to a separate class of deliverability constraints—the absolute quantity of finite surplus, receiving-side capacity and trans-oceanic bandwidth—that lie outside this study's scope as a downstream question.

### Demand-shape operator for AI workloads

AI load is split into inference and training, and only the inference share \(\lambda_{\mathrm{inf}}\) with an intraday rhythm is reshaped by the shape operator, while the training share \(\lambda_{\mathrm{train}}\) acts as a flat baseload, so as not to peak the flat load along with it and overestimate cost. The shape operator redistributes the inference share according to its possible intraday morphology, and this study compares three treatments — spreading the share into a batch baseload at the annual mean, concentrating it into daytime and evening along an empirical intraday curve, and amplifying only the top 1% of intraday peak hours into a spiky burst. In the daytime-concentration scenario, the inference share is redistributed along the intraday shape curve \(w(h(t))\),

\[
d_c^{(P_2)}(t) = (1 - \lambda_{\mathrm{inf}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{train}})\, d_c(t) + \lambda_{\mathrm{inf}}\, \langle d_c \rangle\, w(h(t)) + \lambda_{\mathrm{train}}\, \langle d_c \rangle, \tag{4}\]

where \(w(h(t))\) is normalized to unit mean and fitted from the public BurstGPT v2.0 trace<sup>26</sup>—the inference peak is at about 18:00 local time with a width of about 9 h—and cross-validated against the independent Azure trace<sup>27</sup>, with fitted values and confidence intervals in Supplementary Sections S2 and S4. The full definition of the spiky-burst scenario and its amplification factor, derived from the BurstGPT hourly burst ratio, are given in Supplementary Section S3, and the batch-flattened share is folded into the realistic-mix scenario of Supplementary Section S5. An empirical-shape scenario that adopts the measured intraday curve directly without a Gaussian assumption yields directionally consistent results and quantifies the bias of the Gaussian parameterization, as detailed in Supplementary Section S2.8; the main text reports the realistic-mix scenario, which linearly superposes the flat batch share and the smoothly daytime-shaped inference share by their literature weights so that the multiple load classes coexist, while the event-driven burst is treated separately and not folded into the main case, with shares and definitions given in Supplementary Section S5.

The AI energy share \(\lambda\) is triangulated across multiple sources on a unified TWh basis and split by the inference fraction, with a median trajectory spanning 2025–2050 whose values are given in Supplementary Section S1. Every combination of year, operator and scenario re-solves the portfolio optimization on the perturbed demand and is cross-checked against a station-level basis to confirm that the conclusions do not depend on the basis functions.

### Robustness and reproducibility

The main conclusions are robust to parameter choices — a one-factor sensitivity sweep of each parameter group on the supply, routing and AI-shape sides shows that relaxing latency tolerance and raising the routable share each monotonically lower the matching gap U and the phase-firming premium Π—neither alone is sufficient—while the dependence on AI demand shape remains small throughout, with the sweeps reported in Supplementary Sections S7 and S14. All analyses are implemented in Python, with the sample covering the full-year hourly series of all 104 countries and no observations removed; derived data and code are released under the corresponding statements.

### Phase-firming premium and storage technology scenarios

We report the firming premium of the residual phase gap by the phase-firming premium \(\Pi_c\), the increment over pure wind-and-solar generation cost of firming that gap with storage,

\[ \Pi_c = \frac{\mathrm{LCOE}^{\mathrm{firm}}_c - \mathrm{LCOE}^{\mathrm{gen}}_c}{\mathrm{LCOE}^{\mathrm{gen}}_c} \in [0,\infty). \tag{5}\]

Its absolute basis is the full electricity-supply cost per MWh of digital demand served from each country's own supply. The firming-storage component of that cost is determined by hourly storage-dispatch optimisation rather than by percentile sizing. For each country, a linear program co-optimises the generation overbuild factor, the power and energy capacities of lithium (Li) and long-duration energy storage (LDES) and their hourly charge/discharge, so that the hourly energy balance holds after accounting for round-trip efficiency, state-of-charge (SOC) recursion, annual SOC cycling and curtailment, with unserved load priced at the value of lost load (VOLL) to make reliability endogenous; the objective is minimum annualized full-system cost. The annualized firming-storage cost is the sum over the two technologies,

\[ \mathrm{Storage}_c = \sum_{\mathrm{tech}\in\{\mathrm{Li},\,\mathrm{LDES}\}} (\mathrm{CRF}_{\mathrm{tech}}+\mathrm{FOM})\,\big(p_{\mathrm{tech}}\,P_{\mathrm{tech}} + e_{\mathrm{tech}}\,E_{\mathrm{tech}}\big)\times 10^3, \tag{6}\]

where \(P_{\mathrm{tech}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{tech}}\) are the dispatch-optimised power and energy capacities, and round-trip efficiency enters the physical balance through the SOC recursion; the power and energy costs, round-trip efficiency and lifetime of each technology are given in Supplementary Section S13. The per-kWh cost of the two technologies crosses at a gap duration of about 10 hours, consistent with the DOE definition of long-duration storage. The sensitivity of the full electricity-supply cost to the reliability target, annual cycling and storage-cost scenarios is given in Supplementary Section S16, and the cost advantage of global over local is robust.

Storage costs by year from 2025 to 2050 are taken, for lithium, from NREL utility-scale storage cost projections<sup>28</sup> and the Annual Technology Baseline<sup>29</sup>, and from long-duration storage literature and policy targets<sup>30,31,32</sup>, with conservative / central / optimistic scenarios whose cost-decline rates reference storage experience curves<sup>33,34</sup>, the year-by-year series appearing in Supplementary Section S13. This component is sized endogenously by the dispatch optimisation above and constitutes the firming-storage term in the full electricity-supply cost; of the remaining two terms, generation is accounted using the capacity cost and fixed O&M of NREL ATB 2024 wind and solar (including the capacity built as firming overbuild), and ancillary services are accounted on a per-unit-energy cost of variable renewable generation. With each country's supply fixed domestically and load routed over the internet, the sum of the three terms—generation, firming storage and ancillary services—is the full electricity-supply cost that serves as the absolute basis of each panel of Figure 5. On this basis \(\Pi_c\) (Eq. 5) normalises the firming-storage and ancillary terms to the deployed generation cost. This is the standard normalisation of variable-renewable integration cost to generation cost<sup>11,12</sup>, which bare levelized generation cost systematically understates by ignoring the output timing profile<sup>35</sup>. \(\Pi=0\) corresponds to perfect phase alignment requiring no storage, so \(\Pi_c\), like the matching gap \(U_c\), is dimensionless and anchored at zero at perfect phase alignment; where a common \([0,1)\) scale with \(U_c\) is wanted it can equivalently be written as the firming-cost share \(\sigma_c=\Pi_c/(1+\Pi_c)\). The premium implied here—about 1.61 times generation cost locally and 1.55 times globally—sits within the range IRENA reports for comparable firmed wind-plus-solar delivery<sup>36</sup>. Firming storage is sized by an hour-by-hour state-of-charge (SOC) dispatch optimisation that explicitly represents the state of charge, the charge–discharge sequence, annual energy cycling, curtailment and reliability under a value-of-lost-load penalty; the residual uncertainty is bounded by the reliability-target (99%–100%), annual-cycling and Li/LDES cost ±50% sensitivity ranges reported in Supplementary Section S16.

The source data required to reproduce the five main figures, together with the derived datasets supporting all conclusions of this paper, are deposited in the public repository https://github.com/Lantern567/earth-rotation-cfe . For raw data that cannot be redistributed owing to third-party licence restrictions, this paper provides only the acquisition channels listed above.
